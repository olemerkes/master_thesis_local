\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chapter:introduction}

%\textbf{Establishing of context: What is the HC?}

The Hadley cell (HC) is the dominant meridional large-scale circulation in the tropics. Its ascending branch at the equator is collocated with inter-tropical convergence zone (ITCZ), which is characterized by large amounts of precipitation. On the other hand, the dry, semi-arid and arid regions of subsidence in the subtropics are associated with the descending branch of the HC. In the annual mean and in the equinoxes there are two similar overturning cells in the tropics - one in each hemisphere. It extends from \SIrange{0}{30}{\degree\N} in the Northern hemisphere and equivalently \SIrange{0}{30}{\degree S} in the Southern hemisphere. Throughout the annual cycle there are strong variations in the HC's extent and strength: As the maximum of insolation moves poleward, so does the ITCZ and the HCs, resulting in a strong cross-equatorial winter (hemisphere without the maximum) and a weaker summer HC (hemisphere with the maximum). This seasonal migration is related to the global monsoon, which has been identified as an eddy driven HC regime \parencite{bordoniMonsoonsEddymediatedRegime2008}.\\ 
The HC is responsible for the poleward transport of energy and the equatorward transport of moisture, thereby reducing the meridional temperature gradients imposed by solar insolation. The HC has strong implications for human and animal life as well as agriculture and vegetation on this planet. Its extent crucially modulates global precipitation patterns by setting the regions of ascend and descend. The strength of the HC determines what amount of energy is reaching the off-equatorial region and what amount of moisture is reaching the equator, setting the potential precipitation amounts. This is particularly evident in the Sahel region, which is located approximately \SI{10}{\degree N} of the equator on the African continent. This semi-arid region receives most of its precipitation in the boreal summer when the HC and the ITCZ migrate northward. Thus, the rainfall in the Sahel is dynamically linked to the HC position. Changes in the HC also affect the livelihood of the local population and economies that remain heavily dependent on agricultural production.\\ 
This has also been shown for paleoclimate. Paleoclimate simulations of the HC display large changes in HC strength and extend during the last glacial maximum and the mid holocene due to changes in orbital forcing \parencite{dagostinoFactorsControllingHadley2017}. This is also supported by evidence from tropical paleoclimate proxy records of precipitation changes \parencite{mcgeeChangesITCZLocation2014}.
This past evidence of strong HC changes combined with the large societal impact of precipitation patterns give a strong reason to investigate the projected HC changes of human driven greenhouse gas emissions in the current era.

The \CO{} in the atmosphere is very uniformly distributed. The forcing that it imposes is not. The forcing is critically set by the difference between surface and stratospheric temperature, which varies strongest in the meridional direction. Given that the HC is flattening the meridional energy gradient, the response of the HC strength depends on meridional forcing structure. This has been shown for quadrupling of \CO{} only in certain latitude belts in coupled simulations \parencite{kimWeakHadleyCell2022}. A forcing in mid latitudes weakens the Hadley circulation whereas a forcing at the equator leads to it strengthening. We investigate how the \textit{vertical} structure of CO2 forcing, rather than its horizontal structure, alters atmospheric circulation. We achieve this by running simulations with a General Circulation Model (GCM) while quadrupling \CO{} only in the troposphere and only in the stratosphere using a pre-industrial control tropopause height to separate the two cases. To our knowledge, these experiments have not yet been described by the literature.

Climate simulations from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP), have established a HC weakening and widening under climate change \parencite{luExpansionHadleyCell2007,xiaComparisonTrendsHadley2020}. Explaining this, previous studies have emphasized the importance of drivers related to the troposphere \parencite{chemkeElucidatingMechanismsResponsible2021,fanWeakeningTropicalCirculation2024}. However, historical simulations show that stratospheric forcing also influences the general circulation. In particular stratospheric ozone depletion has led to a cooling of the Southern hemisphere lower-stratospheric polar cap. This has been identified as the main driver for the widening of the Hadley circulation over the second half of the twentieth century \parencite{polvaniStratosphericOzoneDepletion2011}. The goal of our study is to disentangle the contributions of tropospheric and stratospheric \CO{} to the established HC weakening with uniform \CO{} distribution.  
We find that the HC response to tropospheric \CO{} is a strengthening and widening in both hemispheres. This opposes our expectations evoked from the literature, which identifies tropospheric warming parameters as the main drivers for HC weakening under uniform \CO{} concentrations. Our results suggest that the weakening of the HC originates from stratospheric cooling caused by stratospheric \CO{}. To investigate and explain the different responses of the HC strength we employ angular momentum and energy budget frameworks. We use the axisymmetric angular momentum framework to attribute HC changes to changes in tropopause height, meridional temperature, gradient, and vertical stability. Since this theory ignores the contribution of eddies to HC strength we also investigate changes in eddy divergence to explain the observed changes in the mean circulation. The energetic framework is a diagnostically unifying approach. It uses changes in top-of-atmosphere (TOA) radiative fluxes and moist static energy to distinguish different contributing factors to HC changes.

%\textbf{Significance}

We contribute to the advancement of the field by revising the applicability of different HC frameworks and simplified models. Splitting up \CO{} forcing in two vertical layers puts existing theories to the test and gives the opportunity to rethink accepted truths in climate science. Overall, this is a contribution to foundational research that advances our physical understanding of the drivers of general circulation changes.
