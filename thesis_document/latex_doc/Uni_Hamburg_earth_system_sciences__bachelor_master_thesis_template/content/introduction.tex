\textbf{Establishing of context: What is the HC?}

The Hadley cell (HC) is the dominant meridional circulation in the tropics. Its ascending branch at the equator is collocated with inter-tropical convergence zone (ITCZ). The dry regions of subsidence in the subtropics are associated with the descending branch of the HC. In the annual mean there are two overturning cells in the tropics, which extend from \SIrange{0}{30}{\degree\N} in the Northern hemisphere and equivalently \SIrange{0}{30}{\degree S} in the Southern hemisphere. Throughout the annual cycle there are strong variations in the HC's extent and strength: As the maximum of insolation moves poleward, so does the ITCZ and the HCs, resulting in a strong winter and a weaker summer HC. This seasonal migration is related to the global monsoons.\\ 
The HC is responsible for the poleward transport of angular momentum (and energy) and the equatorward transport of moisture, thereby reducing the meridional temperature gradients imposed by solar insolation. Therefore, the HC has strong implications for life on this planet. Its extent crucially modulates global precipitation patterns. The strength of the HC determines the moisture transport of moisture and energy. This is particularly given in the Sahel region. This semi-arid region receives most of its precipitation in the boreal summer when the HC and the ITCZ migrate northward. Thus, the rainfall in the Sahel is dynamically linked to the HC. Changes in the HC also affect the livelihood of the local population and economies which still very much depend on agricultural production. Paleoclimate simulations of the HC show large changes in HC strength and extend during the last glacial maximum and the mid holocene due to changes in orbital forcing \parencite{dagostinoFactorsControllingHadley2017}.This is also supported by evidence from tropical paleoclimate proxy records of precipitation changes \parencite{mcgeeChangesITCZLocation2014}. \\
These paleoclimate records combined with the large societal impact of precipitation patterns give a strong reason to investigate the projected HC changes of human driven greenhouse gas emissions in the current era.

\CO is very uniformly distributed in the atmosphere. The forcing that it imposes is not. The HC strength response depends on meridional forcing structure. This has been shown for quadrupling of \CO only in certain latitude belts \parencite{kimWeakHadleyCell2022}. A forcing in mid latitudes weakens the Hadley circulation whereas a forcing at the equator leads to it strengthening. Thus, we want to investigate how \CO forcing in the vertical alters atmospheric circulation. We achieve this by quadrupling \CO only in the troposphere and only in the stratosphere using a pre-industrial control tropopause height. This approach is completely new and has net yet been described by the literature. Previous studies emphasize the importance of drivers related to the troposphere to explain the HC weakening \parencite{chemkeElucidatingMechanismsResponsible2021,fanWeakeningTropicalCirculation2024}.(This study highlights vertical stability.) However, historical simulations show that also stratospheric forcing influences the general circulation. In particular stratospheric ozone depletion has led to a cooling of the Southern hemisphere lower-stratospheric polar cap. This has been identified as the main driver for the widening of the Hadley circulation over the second half of the twentieth century \parencite{polvaniStratosphericOzoneDepletion2011}.\\  
We find that the HC response to tropospheric \CO is a strengthening and widening in both hemispheres. This opposes our expectations from the literature which sees tropospheric warming parameters as the main drivers for HC weakening with uniform \CO concentrations. Our results suggest that the weakening of the HC originates from stratospheric cooling caused by stratospheric \CO. Our study disentangles the contributions of tropospheric and stratospheric \CO to the established HC weakening with uniform \CO distribution. To investigate the different responses of the HC strength we employ angular momentum and energy budget frameworks. The axisymmetric angular momentum framework to attribute HC changes to changes in tropopause height, meridional temperature, gradient, and vertical stability. Since this theory ignores the contribution of eddies to HC strength we also investigate changes in eddy divergence to explain the observed changes in the mean circulation. The energetic framework uses top-of-atmosphere (TOA) radiative fluxes and moist static energy to distinguish different contribution factors.\\
