\section{The temperature and circulation response}
\subsection{Linearity of decomposition}
When separating the uniform quadrupling of \CO{} into a tropospheric and a stratospheric component in the simulations with ECHAM6, one might be concerned that this decomposition even works. Since the decomposition is physically not possible to realize in the atmosphere due to its well-mixed state, the decomposition could produce non-linear effects. In short: Is the response to uniform forcing \CO{} identical to the added up responses of the atmosphere to a forcing in the troposphere and stratosphere? Is the net circulation response of the atmosphere to \tropo and \strato decomposable into these two components?
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{initial_plots/linear_T.png}
    \caption{The temperature anomalies with a uniform increase of \CO{} concentration, the added \tropo and \strato cases, and their non-linearities as the difference (from left to right). The black line indicates the tropopause height in the PI run.}
    \label{fig:lin_T}
\end{figure}

To find out whether \CO{} in the vertical shows a linear behavior, we compare the temporal and zonal mean temperature anomalies of the \uniform case to the added up anomalies from \tropo and \strato. As one can see in Figure \ref{fig:lin_T} the temperature response is almost completely linear. The absolute difference between the sum of the anomalies of \tropo and \strato minus the anomalies of \uniform have a mean value of \SI{0.2}{\K} and a maximum magnitude of \SI{1.8}{\K}. The largest discrepancies can be found in the Northern hemisphere stratosphere and right above the tropopause in the tropics. Since we are most interested in the response of the atmospheric circulation, we also check its linearity. In Figure \ref{fig:lin_msf}, one can see that the MSF in \uniform very much resembles the sum of \tropo and \strato. Here, the mean absolute difference is about $0.5\%$ with a maximum difference of $6.3\%$ of the maximum absolute value of the MSF in the PI run. (TODO: Do you prefer standard deviation?)

Overall, the decomposition of the \CO{} increase works. The response is approximately linear in the temperature and MSF anomalies. Nonetheless, one should bear the small non-linear effects in mind in the further analysis of these results.
\subsection{The temperature anomalies}
The model simulations for the temperature anomalies in \uniform display quite well the expected patterns established in Section \ref{sec:CO2_T_response}. The temperature throughout the entire troposphere is increased while the warming in the upper tropics is enhanced and amplified in the Arctic. The stratosphere is cooled (see Figure \ref{fig:lin_T} left panel). 
We now expected that the \CO increase in \tropo is responsible for its warming while \CO increase only in the stratosphere is responsible for stratospheric cooling. This is what we see in Figure \ref{fig:temperature_initial}. The warming in the troposphere maintains the spatial structure from the \uniform case. In the response of the \tropo case, we notice an additional cooling in the stratosphere right above the tropopause, which will be further investigated. In \strato the tropospheric temperature remains almost constant. Only right above the tropopause, there is an unexpected anomalous warming. Given these temperature responses, one would still expect the net weakening to originate from tropospheric \CO because the associated warming of the troposphere change the temperature gradients and the tropopause height. However, the warming/cooling right above the tropopause might have an influence on the tropopause height and the vertical stability which have been discussed to result in HC changes. 

To better understand the cause of the anomalous warming in \strato and the anomalous cooling in \tropo right above the tropopause, we perform experiments with \textit{konrad} changing the vertical \CO concentration. In Figure \ref{fig:konrad}, one can see by comparing \uniform to \tropo + \strato that there is also in \textit{konrad} some non-linearity in the response right above the tropopause. However, even when considering the non-linearity there is an anomalous warming above the tropopause in the \strato and an anomalous cooling in the \tropo case. In the experiments with \textit{konrad} the warming and cooling at the tropopause also extends below the tropopause. We believe that the heating rate induced by the stepwise increase in optical thickness associated with the step wise increase of \CO might be its cause. We have tested this hypothesis by linearly increasing \CO on some levels below and above the tropopause, which weakens the anomalous heating pattern. From the experiments with \textit{konrad}, we can conclude that the heating in \strato and cooling in \tropo above the tropopause in the experiments with ECHAM6 are a static thermodynamic response and not evoked by changes in the atmospheric circulation.

\subsection{The atmospheric response}
In this chapter, the circulation response in our experiments is described. What we find when analysing the HC extent according to the definitions established in Section \ref{sec:def_HC} is that the HC is weakening and widening in the \uniform case (see Figure \ref{fig:lin_msf}, left panel) in both hemispheres. This comes as a surprise because one would expect an increase in tropospheric \CO to be the dominant driver here. Although, also the cooling of the stratosphere is expected to change meridional temperature gradients. Figure \ref{fig:msf_initial} shows that the annual mean HC is strengthening with \tropo and weakening with \strato. This suggests that the weakening cannot be traced back to the increase in tropospheric \CO, but to an increase in stratospheric \CO. A further implication might be that the cooling of the stratosphere is responsible for the net weakening of the HC in \uniform and not the warming of the troposphere. For a summary of all HC changes see Table \ref{tab:HC_changes}. One should notice here that the HC strength response is not linear in either hemisphere, while the changes in HC extent are to a good approximation. The non-linearity in the Northern hemisphere accounts for 3.4\% and in the Southern hemisphere for 1.5\%. Considering the magnitude of the individual changes, which are all significantly larger than the HC response to the uniform forcing and the non-linearities, we perceive them as robust physical responses instead of artifacts due to the non-linearities. The HC strength response in \strato is quite different between the hemispheres with the Northern hemisphere weakening almost twice as much as the Southern. In the next section, we identify the underlying physical mechanisms that lead to a strengthening in \tropo and a weakening in \strato employing different HC frameworks.

\section{Physical drivers of HC strength response}
\subsection{Predictions from axisymmetric scalings}
Trying to explain the opposing Hadley cell strength responses but also the HC widening in both cases we use the axisymmetric scalings from Section \ref{sec:axisymmetric}. The following analysis is very much similar to the one from \textcite{seoMechanismFutureChanges2014}. Given the temperature response described above, it is not possible to infer a qualitative explanation of the HC changes from the temperature field using the axisymmetric scalings (see also Section \ref{sec:axisymmetric}). The axisymmetric scalings predict that the HC strength and extent are a function of the tropopause height $H$, the meridional temperature gradient $\Delta_h$, and the vertical stability $\Delta_v$. These parameters are defined according to Section \ref{sec:def}. The individual predictive power of each of these parameters is displayed in Figure \ref{fig:parameters_HH}. For the HC extent anomalies, we find the best correlation ($R^2=0.89$) with the change in vertical stability, a parameter which according to Equation \ref{eq:HH_ext} is not supposed to influence the extent of the HC at all. We find a smaller correlation ($R^2=0.67$) between $\delta_h$ and the HC extent with the tropopause height playing a negligible role ($R^2=0.24$). For the correlations of the parameters with the HC strength we find even less agreement. The largest correlation exists for the tropopause height ($R^2=0.61$). The meridional and vertical temperature gradients show almost no skill at all. The increase in tropopause height in \tropo has the implication that a small part of the "new" troposphere relative to the PI simulation is not affected by the quadrupling of \CO. The same is true for the decrease of tropopause height in the \strato case.
\begin{figure}[H]
    %% trim=left bottom right top,
    \centering
   \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth,keepaspectratio,trim=0 0 0 0, clip]{H&H/parameters_NH_fit.png}
    \caption{Changes in annual mean HC extent and strength against changes in tropopause height $H$, meridional temperature gradient $\delta_h$, and vertical stability $\delta_v$ (top down) for the four experiments. Linear regression shows their agreement.}
    \label{fig:parameters_HH}
\end{figure}
Since the individual parameters cannot significantly predict the HC changes, we now consider their combined contribution. Using the linearisations from Section \ref{sec:lin}, we can calculate what the change in HC would be by just considering the change of the tropopause height, the meridional temperature gradient, and the vertical stability. In Figure \ref{fig:scaling_HH}, one can see the combined contributions according to the axisymmetric scalings. If the scalings hold exactly, all datapoints would lie on the one-to-one line. We see that the changes in the thermodynamical parameters significantly underpredict the simulated changes in HC extent while they overpredict the changes in HC strength. Thus, one can draw the conclusion that the axisymmetric scalings are not suitable to explain the changes in the HC that we see in the simulations.

\subsection{Eddy driven HC changes}
As was reported in Section \ref{sec:theo_eddy}, eddies are known to substantially influence the mean meridional circulation. This has been shown in particular for the HC in its equinox, which is similar to the annual mean HC, and summer regime. Since the eddies are closely related to the baroclinicity in a flow, which itself is driven by meridional temperature gradients, we expect them to change given the very different temperature response in \tropo and \strato. An increase in the meridional temperature gradient strengthens the zonal wind and thus the generation of eddies. In particular, in \tropo the meridional temperature gradient at the height of the subtropical jet stream increases while in \strato it decreases (see Figure \ref{fig:temperature_initial}). Using Equation \eqref{eq:T_edd} and calculating the eddy momentum fluxes as described in Section \ref{sec:meth_eddy}, we can estimate to what extent the HC in the simulations is driven by eddy momentum fluxes and their relative contribution to HC changes. 

Plotting the maximum MSF (HC strength) against the integrated eddy momentum flux $\mathcal{T}$ as in Figure \ref{fig:eddy_flux_strength} reveals that the annual mean Northern hemisphere HC is generally more eddy-driven than its Southern hemisphere counterpart. If the HC would be entirely eddy-driven all datapoints would lie on the one-to-one line. This can be seen for the Ferrel cell (not shown). Changes in eddy momentum flux are similar in both hemispheres. Relative to the PI simulation $\mathcal{T}$ is increasing in \tropo, slightly weakening in \uniform and largely weakening in \strato. The relative changes in $\mathcal{T}$ overpredict the changes in HC strength in the Northern hemisphere \tropo and \strato as well as in the Southern hemisphere \uniform and \strato cases. This indicates that more factors that drive the HC and influence their relative changes should be considered on the analysis. Again, one can notice the non-linearity of the response, with the sum of the changes in $\mathcal{T}$ in \tropo and \strato exceeding the \uniform case. However, performing a linear regression shows a good correlation between the integrated eddy momentum fluxes and the HC strength.

\section{HC changes in the energetic framework}
We will exemplify this analysis on the annual mean Southern hemisphere HC changes step-by-step and only show the full result of the Northern hemisphere in the last part to highlight the differences. We will use the Southern hemisphere because it has a lower OHU contribution which is an artifact from the model setup (fixed sea ice).


Southern hemisphere HC changes are slightly different because the location of the maximum has slightly moved. We stay at the same latitude so that the calculations stay energetically closed.
\subsection{GMS contribution}
The strength of the HC, which is the maximum mass stream flux, is in relation to the energy transport by the HC and its efficiency. The efficiency of energy transport is described by GMS (see Section \ref{sec:theo_energy}). Increasing the required energy transport by the atmosphere while the GMS remains constant increases the HC strength. If the energy transport by the HC remains constant but GMS increases, the HC strength weakens. In this section, we calculate the contribution of changes in HC energy transport $F_\mathrm{HC}$ and GMS $H$ according to Equation \eqref{eq:F_HC} and \eqref{eq:GMS} using the linearised energy budget equation. Figure \ref{fig:GMS_change} shows that in \uniform and \tropo the increase in required energy transport is contribution to a strengthening of the HC while the increase in GMS is contributing to a weakening of the HC. For \uniform the GMS increase contribution prevails leading to a small net weakening of the HC while in \tropo the changes in $F_\mathrm{HC}$ are large leading to the net strengthening of the HC. In \strato we see the opposite response. The energy transport and the GMS are both decreasing. Since the decrease in GMS is smaller than the decrease in energy transport the HC weakens. To find out what drives the changes in we plot the vertical profile of changes in MSE flux weighted by the HC strength at the location of the MSF minimum (see Figure \ref{fig:GMS_vertical}). Vertically integrating these profiles and dividing by the GMS of the PI control yields the changes in GMS. The dominant change in MSE flux occurs at the tropopause. The heating in \strato and cooling in \tropo in the stratosphere does not affect the changes in GMS. Integrating to the tropopause only does not qualitatively change the result. We further decompose the changes in energy transport, to investigate its drivers in the next sections.

\subsection{Eddy contribution}
The energy transport of the HC can change either by changes in total atmospheric energy transport or by changes in energy eddy transport. The eddy term consists of the stationary and transient eddies, because the eddy component is calculated as the residual of the energy transport of the atmosphere minus the transport by the Hadley cell.  The more energy transport by the eddies, the less has to be performed by the mean circulation given the same amount of total energy transport by the atmosphere. Figure \ref{fig:eddy_change} shows that the energy transport by the eddy energy fluxes increases contributing to a weakening of the HC in all experiments. The increase is largest in \uniform (17.5\%), slightly smaller (13.8\%) in \tropo and very small (2.6\%) in \strato. Connecting the increase in eddy energy transport to changes in meridional temperature structure proves difficult. For \uniform and \tropo, Figure \ref{fig:jet_change} clearly shows an increase in the meridional temperature gradient and the subtropical jet strength, which also increases its baroclinicity ,i.e., the generation of eddies. For \strato, there is a decrease of the mean zonal wind right at the tropopause while it is increasing in the surrounding areas.

\subsection{OHU contribution}
In our experiments we are using an slab-ocean configuration with a prescribed energy flux at the surface. Thus, one does not expect any changes on ocean heat transport. When investigating the surface fluxes (net LW, net SW, latent heat, sensible heat, see Figure \ref{fig:ohu_seaice}), we find that this is not true for the simulated time period. The culprit is the sea ice. The anomalies in surface heat fluxes have an identical spatial structure. Since the sea ice stays fixed in our simulations the SSTs below can only warm through conduction. With the air temperature above the sea ice remain much larger than the SSTs below the sea ice there is a constant flux of energy out of the atmosphere into the ocean. This affects the global atmospheric circulation because the removal of energy in higher latitudes requires additional poleward energy transport by the atmosphere. Running the simulations for a longer period until the temperatures above and below sea ice are in equilibrium should remove this effect. Calculating difference between the energy transport by the atmosphere and the total energy transport from TOA radiative fluxes, we obtain the OHU contribution. Additional poleward transport requires a strengthening of the HC, which is why the OHU contributes positively in \uniform and \tropo in Figure \ref{fig:ohu}. In \strato, this effect is negligible because the troposphere is not warming. There is no temperature contrast between the surface air temperature above and the SST below the sea ice. This effect is much stronger in the Northern hemisphere because there is more sea ice, and more warming of the air close to the surface in the Arctic than in the Antarctic.

\subsection{Forcing and feedback contributions}
The changes in total energy transport $F_\mathrm{ao}$ are calculated from the TOA radiative imbalance $R_\mathrm{TOA}$. This can be split up into radiative flux changes due to changes in surface temperature (feedback component) and due to the direct effect of \CO (forcing). As forcing we will consider the net energy input (NEI) into atmosphere in the fixed SST simulations. It can be calculated as the difference between fluxes TOA and at the surface. The meridional structure of the forcing and the feedbacks can also contribute to HC changes. Is the forcing larger in the tropics compared to the extratropics requires additional poleward energy transport. This is the case in \uniform and \strato in the Southern hemisphere (see Figure \ref{fig:forcing}). The spatial structure of the forcing is primarily driven by the distribution of land masses. Since the land cannot take up heat but the oceans can the areas over the ocean take up more heat than enters the atmospheric column ($R_\mathrm{surf}>R_\mathrm{TOA}$) while the energy that areas over land receive is also partly taken up by the ocean ($R_\mathrm{surf}<R_\mathrm{TOA}$). In \uniform and \tropo the continental land masses of Southern Africa, Oceania, and South America are responsible for the net surplus of energy relative to the Southern hemisphere mid-latitudes. In \strato this effect is particularly large in the Antarctic, which is why the forcing contributes to a weakening of the HC here. The full decomposition can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:contributions_SH}. Changes in due to feedback only contribute to circulation changes in \uniform and \tropo. They contribute to a strengthening of the HC, which has its cause in the structure of the water vapor, and possibly cloud feedbacks. In \strato there are almost no changes in surface temperature which is reflected in its low contribution. A further decomposition in the individual feedbacks requires the radiative kernel technique \parencite{sodenQuantifyingClimateFeedbacks2008}.

\subsection{Quantifying HC change contributions for the Northern hemisphere}
When comparing previously discussed drivers of the HC changes in the Southern hemisphere to the Northern hemisphere, one can notice that the contributions are quite different. While the forcing contributed positively to the HC strength in \uniform and \tropo in the Southern hemisphere, it contributes to a weakening in the Northern hemisphere. Figure \ref{fig:forcing} shows that the large increase in the forcing in mid-latitudes is caused by the large Eurasian and North American land mass. The contributions of the feedbacks stays positive except for the \strato case, where it causes a small weakening. For the contribution of GMS, \tropo is much less positive while \strato is much more positive. Here, we can also see the largest non-linearity of the sum of \tropo and \strato which might als cause the large net linearity in the HC changes. For the eddies, we notice that the increase in \tropo exceeds the one in \uniform, which was not the case in the Southern hemisphere. The contribution of \strato is even positive, which can be explained by the stronger weakening of the zonal mean zonal winds in the Northern hemisphere, which can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:jet_change}. The increased OHU has a much larger HC strengthening contribution in the Northern hemisphere. As one can see in Figure \ref{fig:ohu_seaice}, can this be explained by the the larger sea ice fraction and the larger warming in the Arctic. While the net response in HC strength is qualitatively similar in the Northern and Southern hemisphere, the underlying physical mechanism of the response differ significantly.
