\chapter{Results}
\label{chapter:results}
This chapter presents the results of our simulations. The first part covers the temperature and atmospheric circulation response in our experiments, while the second part identifies the mechanism behind the HC responses.
\section{The temperature and circulation response}
\subsection{Linearity of the decomposition}
% When separating the uniform quadrupling of \CO{} into a tropospheric and a stratospheric component in the simulations with ECHAM6, one might be concerned that this decomposition even works. Since the decomposition is physically not possible to realize in the atmosphere due to its well-mixed state, the decomposition could produce non-linear effects. In short: Is the response to uniform forcing \CO{} identical to the added up responses of the atmosphere to a forcing in the troposphere and stratosphere? Is the net circulation response of the atmosphere to \tropo and \strato decomposable into these two components?
\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{initial_plots/linear_T.png}
    \caption{The temperature anomalies with a uniform increase of \CO{} concentration, the added \tropo and \strato cases, and their non-linearities as the difference (from left to right). The black line indicates the tropopause height in the PI run.}
    \label{fig:lin_T}
\end{figure}

To verify that the vertical separation of two different \CO{} concentrations does not show any non-linear behavior, we compare the temporal and zonal mean temperature anomalies of the \uniform case to the added up anomalies from \tropo and \strato. As one can see in Figure \ref{fig:lin_T}, the non-linear effects in the temperature response are really small. The absolute difference between the sum of the anomalies of \tropo and \strato minus the anomalies of \uniform have a mean value of \SI{0.2}{\K} and a maximum magnitude of \SI{1.8}{\K}. The largest discrepancies can be found in the northern hemisphere stratosphere and right above the tropopause in the tropics.

We find a similar behavior for the circulation response. In Figure \ref{fig:lin_msf}, one can see that the MSF in \uniform very much resembles the sum of \tropo and \strato. Here, the mean absolute difference is about $0.5\%$ with a maximum difference of $6.3\%$ of the maximum absolute value of the MSF in the PI run.

Overall, the decomposition of the \CO{} increase works. The response is approximately linear in the temperature and MSF anomalies. Nonetheless, one should bear the small non-linear effects in mind in the further analysis of these experiments.
\subsection{Tropospheric warming and stratospheric cooling can be decomposed}\label{subsec:temperature_anomalies}
Figure \ref{fig:lin_T}shows that the temperature anomalies in \uniform display quite well the expected patterns established in Section \ref{sec:CO2_T_response}. The temperature throughout the entire troposphere is increased while the warming in the upper tropics is enhanced and amplified in the Arctic. The stratosphere is cooled.

We now expected that the \CO increase in \tropo is responsible for the warming of the troposphere, whereas the \CO increase only in the stratosphere is responsible for stratospheric cooling. This is what we see in Figure \ref{fig:temperature_initial}. The warming in the troposphere in \tropo maintains the spatial structure from the \uniform case. Additionally, in \tropo one can notice an additional cooling in the stratosphere right above the tropopause. Besides the cooling of the stratosphere,in \strato the tropospheric temperature remains almost constant. Only right above the tropopause, there is an unexpected anomalous warming.  This warming/cooling right above the tropopause might have an influence on the tropopause height and the vertical stability, which have been discussed to result in HC changes. 

To better understand the cause of the anomalous warming in \strato and the anomalous cooling in \tropo right above the tropopause, we perform experiments with \textit{konrad} changing the vertical \CO concentration. In Figure \ref{fig:konrad}, one can see by comparing \uniform to \tropo + \strato that there is also in \textit{konrad} some non-linearity in the response right above the tropopause. However, even when considering the non-linearity there is an anomalous warming above the tropopause in the \strato and an anomalous cooling in the \tropo case. In the experiments with \textit{konrad} the warming and cooling at the tropopause also extends below the tropopause. The anomalous heating rates arising at and around the level of discontinuous \CO concentrations are induced by the stepwise increase in optical thickness associated with the step wise increase of \CO might be its cause. We have tested this hypothesis by linearly increasing \CO on some levels below and above the tropopause, which weakens the anomalous heating pattern. From the experiments with \textit{konrad}, we can conclude that the heating in \strato and cooling in \tropo above the tropopause in the experiments with ECHAM6 are a static thermodynamic response and not evoked by changes in the atmospheric circulation.

\subsection{Opposing Hadley circulation responses}


In our GCM experiment with a uniform increase in \CO concentration, we manage to reproduce the widening and the weakening of the annual mean HC (see Figure \ref{fig:lin_msf}, left panel) established in Section \ref{sec:HC_proj}. The widening has a magnitude of \SI{1.6}{\deg} in both hemispheres. The weakening is 0.6\% stronger in the southern than in the northern hemisphere, where it has decreased by 1.9\% compared to its PI value.

Figure \ref{fig:msf_initial} shows opposing HC strength responses in \tropo and \strato. We find that the weakening of the Hadley circulation in \uniform is caused by the increase in stratospheric \CO. In \strato the HC is weakening by 8.9\% and 5.9\% in the northern and southern hemisphere, respectively. In contrast, the HC is strengthening in both hemispheres with an increase in tropospheric \CO. This suggests that the net weakening of the HC in \uniform is not caused by the increase in tropospheric \CO, but by an increase in stratospheric \CO. A further implication might be that the cooling of the stratosphere is responsible for the net weakening of the HC in \uniform and not the warming of the troposphere.

The HC extent response is very similar in \tropo and \strato. In both experiments, the HC is widening in northern and southern hemisphere. The partitioning is equal in the northern hemisphere, where the HC is widening by \SI{0.7}{\deg} in \tropo and \strato. However, in the southern hemisphere the widening is stronger in \tropo than in \strato. Table \ref{tab:HC_changes} provides a summary of all HC changes in our experiments.


% Given the temperature responses in \tropo and \strato, one would still expect the net weakening to originate from tropospheric \CO because the associated warming of the troposphere change the temperature gradients and the tropopause height. What we find when analysing the HC extent according to the definitions established in Section \ref{sec:def_HC} is that the HC is weakening and widening in the \uniform case (see Figure \ref{fig:lin_msf}, left panel) in both hemispheres. This comes as a surprise because one would expect an increase in tropospheric \CO to be the dominant driver here. Although, also the cooling of the stratosphere is expected to change meridional temperature gradients. Figure \ref{fig:msf_initial} shows that the annual mean HC is strengthening with \tropo and weakening with \strato. This suggests that the weakening cannot be traced back to the increase in tropospheric \CO, but to an increase in stratospheric \CO. A further implication might be that the cooling of the stratosphere is responsible for the net weakening of the HC in \uniform and not the warming of the troposphere. For a summary of all HC changes see Table \ref{tab:HC_changes}.

While the changes in HC extent combine linearly to the uniform forcing response, one should notice here that the HC strength response is not exactly linear in either hemisphere. The non-linearity in the northern hemisphere accounts for 3.4\% and in the southern hemisphere for 1.5\%. Despite the deviations from exact linearity, the qualitative picture is very clear. In our experiments, stratospheric \CO forcing causes a HC weakening, which is then partially offset due to strengthening in response to tropospheric \CO, adding up to the net weakening in \uniform. Because this result is largely unexpected, we will identify the underlying physical mechanisms that lead to a strengthening in \tropo and a weakening in \strato employing different HC frameworks, in the next section.

\section{Physical drivers of HC strength response}
\subsection{Predictions from axisymmetric scalings}
Trying to explain the opposing Hadley cell strength responses but also the HC widening in both cases we use the axisymmetric scalings from Section \ref{sec:axisymmetric}. The following analysis is very much similar to the one from \textcite{seoMechanismFutureChanges2014}. The axisymmetric scalings predict that the HC strength and extent are a function of the tropopause height $H$, the meridional temperature gradient $\Delta_h$, and the vertical stability $\Delta_v$. Given the temperature response described above, it is not possible to infer a qualitative explanation of the HC changes from the qualitative changes in the scaling parameters (see also Section \ref{sec:axisymmetric}). 

The individual predictive power of each of these parameters is displayed in Figure \ref{fig:parameters_HH}. For the HC extent anomalies, we find the best correlation ($R^2=0.60$) with the change in vertical stability, a parameter which according to Equation \ref{eq:HH_ext} is not supposed to influence the extent of the HC at all. We find even smaller correlation of $\delta_h$, and the the tropopause height $H$ with the HC extent.

For the correlations of the parameters with the HC strength, we find a slightly better agreements. A good correlation exists for the tropopause height ($R^2=0.64$) and the meridional temperature gradient. The vertical temperature gradients show almost no skill at all in predicting the HC extent.

The increase in tropopause height in \tropo has the implication that a small part of the "new" troposphere relative to the PI simulation is not affected by the quadrupling of \CO. The same is true for the decrease of tropopause height in the \strato case.

    \caption{Changes in annual mean HC extent and strength against changes in tropopause height $H$, meridional temperature gradient $\delta_h$, and vertical stability $\delta_v$ (top down) for the four experiments. Linear regression shows their agreement.}
    \label{fig:parameters_HH}
\end{figure}

Since the individual parameters cannot sufficiently predict the HC changes, we now consider their combined contribution. Using the linearizations from Section \ref{sec:lin}, we can calculate what the change in HC would be by just considering the change of the tropopause height, the meridional temperature gradient, and the vertical stability. In Figure \ref{fig:scaling_HH}, one can see the combined contributions according to the axisymmetric scalings. If the scalings hold exactly, all datapoints would lie on the one-to-one line. We see that the changes in the thermodynamical parameters significantly underpredict the simulated changes in HC extent while they overpredict the changes in HC strength. Thus, the axisymmetric scalings are not suitable to explain the changes in the HC that we see in the simulations.

Doing the same analysis for the southern hemisphere (not shown), we find different slopes and correlations but not an overall increase in predictive skill of the axisymmetric scalings. Also when investigating the winter HCs, which, according to the dynamical framework, are closer to angular momentum conservation, the results do not change qualitatively. Finally, we suspect in particular the influence of the eddies to fundamentally influence the strength of the HC, which are neglected in the axisymmetric framework. Thus, we investigate the changes in eddy momentum flux in the next section.
\subsection{Eddy driven HC changes}\label{subsec:eddy_driven}
As was reported in Section \ref{sec:theo_eddy}, eddies are known to substantially influence the mean meridional circulation. This has been shown in particular for the HC in its equinox, which is similar to the annual mean HC, and summer regime. The flow in our simulations has a maximum Rossby number of about 0.3, which favors this perspective over the axisymmetric one. Since the eddy fluxes are closely related to the baroclinicity in a flow, which itself is driven by meridional temperature gradients, we expect them to change, given the very different temperature response in \tropo and \strato. An increase in the meridional temperature gradient strengthens the zonal wind and thus the generation of eddies. In particular, in \tropo the meridional temperature gradient at the height of the subtropical jet stream increases while in \strato it decreases (see Figure \ref{fig:temperature_initial}). Using Equation \eqref{eq:T_edd} and calculating the eddy momentum fluxes as described in Section \ref{sec:meth_eddy}, we can estimate to what extent the HC in the simulations is driven by eddy momentum fluxes and their relative contribution to HC changes. 

Plotting the maximum MSF (HC strength) against the integrated eddy momentum flux $\mathcal{T}$ as in Figure \ref{fig:eddy_flux_strength} reveals that the annual mean northern hemisphere HC is generally more eddy-driven than its southern hemisphere counterpart. If the HC was entirely eddy-driven, all datapoints would lie on the one-to-one line. This can be seen for the Ferrel cell (not shown). Changes in eddy momentum flux are similar in both hemispheres. Relative to the PI simulation $\mathcal{T}$ is increasing in \tropo, slightly weakening in \uniform and largely weakening in \strato. The relative changes in $\mathcal{T}$ overpredict the changes in HC strength in the northern hemisphere \tropo and \strato as well as in the southern hemisphere \uniform and \strato cases. This indicates that more factors that drive the HC and influence their relative changes should be considered on the analysis. Again, one can notice the non-linearity of the response, with the sum of the changes in $\mathcal{T}$ in \tropo and \strato exceeding the \uniform case. While the integrated eddy momentum fluxes and the HC strength are well correlated, the slope and the offset of the linear regression is inconsistent with the predictions from Equation \eqref{eq:T_edd}, indicating that the advection of zonal mean angular momentum from the axisymmetric scaling can also not be neglected to get an exact result. This agrees with $Ro\neq0$ and highlights the necessity for a unifying approach.

\section{Energetic perspective on the HC strength response}
The analysis using angular momentum budget presented in the previous sections is not able to explain, why tropospheric \CO strengthens and stratospheric \CO weakens the HC. The simulated annual mean HC is neither in the mean angular momentum conserving limit, nor in the eddy-driven limit. For intermediate regimes, there does not exist a dynamical framework. However, the energy budget framework provides a diagnostic perspective on HC strength changes. In the following, we will decompose the HC strength changes into contributions from changes in GMS, energy eddy fluxes, OHU, radiative forcing, and climate feedbacks. We demonstrate the method for the southern hemisphere, where the artificial ocean heat uptake caused by the fixed sea ice is smaller (see Section \ref{subsec:ohu}), and discuss the northern hemisphere afterwards. Southern hemisphere HC changes are slightly changed ($\sim0.5\%$ in \tropo) in the following analysis compared to previous sections, because the latitude of the maximum has slightly moved. This prevents a useful comparison of the different \CO forcing cases.


\subsection{Gross moist stability contribution}
The strength of the HC, which is defined by the maximum mass stream flux, is directly related to the energy transport by the HC and its efficiency ($\psi_\mathrm{max}=F_\mathrm{HC}/\mathcal{H}$). The efficiency of energy transport is described by GMS (see Section \ref{sec:theo_energy}). Increasing the required energy transport by the atmosphere, while the GMS remains constant, also increases the HC strength. If the energy transport by the HC remains constant, but the efficiency of energy transport by the HC $\mathcal{H}$ increases, the HC strength weakens. In this section, we calculate the contribution of changes in HC energy transport $F_\mathrm{HC}$ and GMS $\mathcal{H} $ according to Equation \eqref{eq:F_HC} and \eqref{eq:GMS} using the linearized energy budget equation.

The more energy transport by the eddies, the less has to be performed by the mean circulation given the same amount of total energy transport by the atmosphere.

Figure \ref{fig:contributions_SH} shows that the energy transport by the eddy energy flux increases, thereby contributing to a weakening of the HC in all experiments. \subsection{Ocean heat uptake contribution} \label{subsec:ohu}
In our experiments, we are using an slab-ocean configuration with a prescribed energy flux at the surface. Thus, one does not expect any changes on ocean heat transport. When investigating the surface fluxes (net LW, net SW, latent heat, sensible heat, see Figure \ref{fig:ohu_seaice}), we find that this is not true for the simulated time period.

The culprit is the sea ice. Looking at Figure \ref{fig:ohu_seaice}, one can see that the anomalies in surface heat fluxes have an identical spatial structure as the sea ice extent. Since the sea ice stays fixed in our simulations the SSTs below can only warm through conduction. With the air temperature above the sea ice remaining much larger than the SSTs below the sea ice. There is a constant flux of energy out of the atmosphere into the ocean. This affects the global atmospheric circulation, because the removal of energy in higher latitudes requires additional poleward energy transport by the atmosphere. Running the simulations for a longer period until the temperatures above and below sea ice are in equilibrium should remove this effect. 

Additional poleward transport as provided by the anomalous surface fluxes, requires a strengthening of the HC, which is why the OHU contributes positively in \uniform and \tropo in Figure \ref{fig:contributions_SH}. Because the troposphere is not warming and there is no temperature contrast between the surface air temperature above and the SST below the sea ice, this effect is negligible in \strato. The effect is small compared to the other contributions, but still on the order of the total change. It dampens the weakening response in \uniform, which would show a stronger weakening otherwise. Its contribution might alone cause the net strengthening of the HC in \tropo.

\subsection{Radiative forcing and feedback contributions}
% The changes in total energy transport $F_\mathrm{ao}$ are calculated from the TOA radiative imbalance $R_\mathrm{TOA}$. The radiative imbalance can be split up into radiative flux changes due to changes in surface temperature (feedback component) and due to the direct effect of \CO (forcing). As forcing we will consider the net energy input (NEI) into atmosphere in the fixed SST simulations. It can be calculated as the difference between fluxes TOA and at the surface. Other studies only use the TOA radiative imbalance here. The meridional structure of the forcing and the feedbacks can also contribute to HC changes. A larger forcing in the tropics compared to the extratropics requires additional poleward energy transport.
The forcing and the feedback contributions determine, how much energy has to be transported in the first place. This is set by the meridional structure, with an energy excess in the tropics relative to the higher latitudes increasing the strength of the HC, while a deficiency weakens it.

Figure \ref{fig:forcing} shows that the spatial structure of the forcing is primarily caused by the distribution of land masses: Since the land cannot take up heat but the oceans can, the areas over the ocean take up more heat than enters the atmospheric column ($R_\mathrm{surf}>R_\mathrm{TOA}$), while the energy that areas over land receive is also partly taken up by the ocean ($R_\mathrm{surf}<R_\mathrm{TOA}$). Fixing also the land temperatures increases this effect, because there would be even less OLR at TOA increasing the imbalance. In \uniform and \tropo the continental land masses of southern Africa, Oceania, and South America are responsible for the zonal mean net surplus of energy in the tropics relative to the southern hemisphere mid-latitudes, leading to the positive contributions in Figure \ref{fig:contributions_SH}. The large surplus in the Antarctic is distorted by the area convergence at the poles. In \strato the effect is damped in the tropics, which is why the forcing contributes to a weakening of the HC here. 

% The full decomposition of HC strength drivers for the southern hemisphere can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:contributions_SH}. 
In \uniform and \tropo the feedback contribution, which is calculated as the residual ($F_\mathrm{fdb}=F_\mathrm{ao}-F_\mathrm{forc}$), leads to a strengthening of the HC. This might have its cause in the tropic amplified meridional structure of the water vapor, and possibly cloud feedbacks \parencite{feldlCharacterizingHadleyCirculation2016,kimWeakHadleyCell2022}. In \strato there are almost no changes in surface temperature, which is reflected in its low transport induction.
% A further decomposition in the individual feedbacks requires the radiative kernel technique \parencite{sodenQuantifyingClimateFeedbacks2008}.

In the southern hemisphere of \uniform, the forcing, feedback, and OHU contribute to a strengthening of the HC with the feedbacks having the largest weight. Still, there is a net reduction in HC strength, which is caused by the increase in GMS and eddy energy transport.
For \tropo, we find very much similar contributions as in the \uniform case. The largest differences between these two can be found in the radiative forcing and eddy contributions, which adds up to the net strengthening of the HC in the \tropo case. The signal in \strato is generally much smaller for each of the contributor. Most of this can be explained by the absence of a temperature response in the troposphere. The largest contribution to the net weakening originates from the radiative forcing contribution. The residual remains small for all cases. 

The response of the contributions is approximately linear. Despite the discussed non-linearities in the temperature and circulation responses, the sum of \tropo and \strato contribution yields the \uniform contribution. This gives good reason to understand its changes as the sum of stratospheric and tropospheric \CO.

\subsection{Quantifying HC change contributions for the northern hemisphere}
Major differences can be found when comparing the previously discussed contributions to the HC changes in the southern hemisphere to the northern hemisphere (see Figure \ref{fig:contributions_NH}). The forcing contributed positively to the HC strength in \uniform and \tropo in the southern hemisphere,whereas it contributes to a weakening in the northern hemisphere. Figure \ref{fig:forcing} shows that the large increase of net energy input into in mid-latitudes is produced by the large Eurasian and North American land mass. The contributions of the feedbacks are, as in the southern hemisphere. positive. Only in the \strato case, it causes a small weakening. For the contribution of GMS, \tropo is much less positive while \strato is much more positive. Here, we can also see the largest non-linearity of the sum of \tropo and \strato, which might also cause the large net linearity in the HC changes. For the eddies, we notice that the increase in \tropo exceeds the one in \uniform, which was not the case in the southern hemisphere. In contrast, the eddy contribution of \strato is positive, which can be explained by the stronger weakening of the zonal mean zonal winds in the northern hemisphere (see Figure \ref{fig:jet_change}). The increased OHU has a much larger HC strengthening contribution in the northern hemisphere. As one can see in Figure \ref{fig:ohu_seaice}, this can be explained by the the larger sea ice fraction and the larger warming in the Arctic, which strengthens the temperature difference between the air and the sea. The contribution to a strengthening of the HC in \tropo even outweigh its net strengthening. This means, that without the OHU in our simulations, we would not find a strengthening of the HC in the northern hemisphere. However, the OHU component is even larger in \uniform, which show a weakening of the HC strength. In summary, the net response in HC strength is qualitatively similar in the northern and southern hemisphere, the underlying physical mechanism of the response differ significantly.
