\section{Model setup}\label{sec:setup}
To investigate the HC response under different vertical \CO forcings, we used an atmospheric circulation model to simulate their influence on the circulation response. To understand the resulting temperature profiles, we used simulations from the RCE model \textit{konrad} as verification. The HC response is very much dependent on the vertical structure of the \CO concentration. We use the different HC frameworks discussed above to diagnose the physical drivers of HC changes. Here, we will describe the specific model setup and the evaluation metrics that we used.  
\subsection{The atmosphere model ECHAM6} \label{subsec:meth_ECHAM}
For our experiments, we use ECHAM 6 \parencite{stevensAtmosphericComponentMPIM2013}, a CMIP 6 type atmospheric General Circulation Model (GCM), coupled to a slab ocean with a depth of $d=\SI{50}{\m}$. In the version T63L47, the model has a vertical resolution of 47 pressure levels on a $\SI{1.875}{\deg}\times\SI{1.875}{\deg}$ ($192\times96$) grid.
We used this complex configuration with continents, orography, water cycle etc. to obtain a realistic circulation response. We chose the slab ocean setup over a dynamical ocean so that the simulations reach equilibrium faster. This model version's spatial resolution is a compromise of accuracy and speed. It is sufficiently small to correctly resolve the circulation in the atmosphere and large enough to not result in unnecessary long runtimes. We prescribed a climatological q-flux that mimics the ocean energy transport of a coupled control run. We fixed the sea ice to avoid problems due to the fixed energy flux in the slab ocean, which would conflict with latent heat release with phase changes of the melting ice. We expected the sea ice to not affect the tropical circulation. To better isolate the effects of \CO in the simulations, one would run more idealized simulations, such as in an aquaplanet setup. The experiments presented here can be seen as experiments of opportunity, which means that they show an interesting result, i.e., opposing HC strength responses, which we want to understand, and not the most ideal setup to test our hypothesis.

We ran four simulations: First, we simulated a pre-industrial control (PI) climate. In the second, we abruptly quadrupled the \CO{} concentration throughout the entire atmosphere (\uniform). Using the climatology tropopause height (see Section \ref{sec:def} for its definition), we only abruptly quadrupled \CO{} in the troposphere (\tropo) in the third, and in all the layers above the tropopause (\strato) while the \CO{} concentration in the remaining domain stays at the PI level in the fourth experiment. The \CO{} concentrations of the four experiments is summarized in Figure \ref{fig:co2_conc_setup}. To calculate the radiative forcing on the atmosphere, we performed identical simulations as in the slab ocean setup while fixing the sea surface temperature (SST) to pre-industrial levels. The goal was here to distinguish between the direct effect of an increase in \CO in the energetic framework on the HC versus the effect of increasing global temperatures. In our experiments the land is still able to warm but this only accounts for a small amount of the net forcing. Also fixing land temperatures increases the net forcing by \SI{1}{\W\m^2} compared to only fixed SSTs and fixed sea ice \parencite{andrewsEffectiveRadiativeForcing2021}.

For all analyses we used yearly mean data. The first ten years of every simulation are discarded as spin-up. The data in PI run is then averaged over a period of 30 years, \uniform over 40 years, and \tropo and \strato over 50 years. 
\subsection{The radiative convective equilibrium model konrad}
To understand the temperature response in the GCM simulations, we used the RCE model \textit{konrad} \parencite{Kluft_konrad}. It solves the RCE in one dimension, which is why it does not account for changes in the atmospheric circulation or horizontal inhomogeneities. As in the experiments with the GCM, we ran four experiments with the one-dimensional vertical separation of \CO{} concentrations as in Figure \ref{fig:co2_conc_setup}. In these simulations, we used the level of the cold point temperature, which is the coldest point in the atmospheric column, as tropopause height. As the surface boundary we used a slab-ocean, which absorbs the TOA radiative imbalance of the PI run in \textit{konrad}, which allows the surface temperature to adjust.









\section{Diagnostics} \label{sec:def}
\subsection{Hadley cell strength and extents}\label{sec:def_HC}
The HC can be characterized by its strength and extent, both of which can be determined by calculating the meridional mass stream function (MSF)
\begin{equation}\label{eq:msf}
    \psi(\phi,p)=\frac{2\pi a\cos\phi}{g}\int_0^{p}\bar{v}\mathrm{d}p'
\end{equation}
with the radius of the Earth $a$, the latitude $\phi$, the gravitational acceleration $g$, the pressure $p$, and the zonal mean meridional wind $\bar{v}$.
The MSF is always parallel to the flow in the vertical $p$--$\phi$ plane (see Figure \ref{fig:HC_seasonal}). A positive value of $\psi_\mathrm{max}$ indicates a clockwise circulation and a negative value a counter-clockwise circulation. In the annual mean, the northern hemisphere shows a clockwise and the southern hemisphere counter-clockwise circulation. Since the MSF vertically integrates the mass flux, its values symbolize the net magnitude of mass transport above and below and to the right and the left of a certain grid point. At the HC maximum the integral covers exactly the upper or lower branch of the circulation. We will use the maximum and minimum of annual-mean stream function between $\SI{40}{\deg S}$ and $\SI{40}{\deg N}$, and between $\SIrange{200}{900}{hPa}$ for the northern and southern hemisphere HC, respectively. In the literature, one can find several different metrics for the HC strength. Some use the MSF at a fixed pressure level or spatially average, while others use the minimum of the vertical pressure tendencies $\omega$. HC trends have shown a large sensitivity to the metric used \parencite{pikovnikMetricsHadleyCirculation2022}.
The HC extent $\phi_\mathrm{H}$, we compute by linear interpolation the subtropical zero-crossing latitude of $\psi$ averaged between the $\SIrange{300}{700}{hPa}$ levels.
All anomalies for a quantity $A$, such as the MSF $\psi$ or the tropopause height $H$, if not mentioned differently, are calculated as 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:anomalies}
    \frac{\delta A}{A}=\frac{A-A_\mathrm{PI}}{A_\mathrm{PI}}. 
\end{equation}

% The global HC, as depicted in Figure \ref{fig:HC_seasonal}, cannot be found at any given longitude but rather there are different regional HCs, which are strongly influenced by the shape and the distribution of continental land mass. HC trends might be different depending on the region of the HC that is considered. This study will only consider the global HC and, if not stated differently, its annual mean.
\subsection{The axisymmetric scaling parameters}\label{sec:lin}
To investigate the drivers of HC changes according to our axisymmetric theory from Section \ref{sec:axisymmetric}, we linearize Equation \eqref{eq:HH_ext} and Equation \eqref{eq:HH_strength} to
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\delta\phi_\mathrm{H}}{\phi_\mathrm{H}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta H}{H}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta\Delta_h}{\Delta_h},
\end{equation}
and  
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\delta\psi_\mathrm{max}}{\psi_\mathrm{max}}=\frac{5}{2}\frac{\delta H}{H}+\frac{5}{2}\frac{\delta\Delta_h}{\Delta_h}-\frac{\delta\Delta_v}{\Delta_v}
\end{equation}

assuming that only the tropopause height $H$, the meridional temperature gradient $\Delta_h$, and the vertical stability $\Delta_v$ change in our simulations while all other parameters remain constant. We define these parameters in our model simulation as the following: The tropopause height $H$ is at the lowest level at which the lapse rate decreases to $\SI{2}{\degreeCelsius\per\km}$ or less, provided also that the average lapse rate between this level and all higher levels within $\SI{2}{\km}$ does not exceed $\SI{2}{\degreeCelsius\per\km}$ \parencite{worldmeteorologicalorganizationInternationalMeteorologicalVocabulary1992}. The result was then averaged over $\SI{15}{\deg S}$ to $\SI{15}{\deg N}$.

For the definition of the meridional temperature gradient $\Delta_h$ and vertical temperature gradient $\Delta_v$ we follow \textcite{dagostinoFactorsControllingHadley2017}. We use the meridional near-surface potential temperature contrast between the deep tropics (equatorward of $\SI{10}{\deg})$ and the extratropics ($\SIrange{40}{60}{\deg}$) averaged over $\SIrange{700}{800}{hPa}$ as $\Delta_h$. For $\Delta_v$, we use the potential temperature difference between $\SI{700}{hPa}$ and $\SI{800}{hPa}$ averaged between $\SIrange{20}{40}{\deg}$.
The results have been tested also with slightly different definitions, which did not change the results qualitatively. 

\subsection{The integrated eddy momentum flux divergence}\label{sec:meth_eddy}
For the eddy-driven HC regime we have to obtain the integrated eddy momentum flux $\mathcal{T}$ from Equation \eqref{eq:T_edd}, we first have to calculate the covariances $\overline{u'v'}$, $\overline{u'\omega'}$ from 6 hourly output data. For this 6 hourly data, we only have an averaging period of 10 years after spin-up. The 6 hourly output is necessary because this is the time scale, on which the eddies occur that influence the mean meridional circulation. Because we use this slightly different dataset also the changes in HC strength and extent are slightly different.
To calculate these, it holds that
\begin{equation*}
    %<a'b'>=<ab>-<a><b>
    \overline{a'b'}=\overline{ab}-\overline{a}\overline{b}
\end{equation*}
with the overline indicating monthly and zonally mean values. To make sure that our eddies do not consist of seasonality, we have to calculate them for each month individually and then average over all months to get the annual mean data. The integrated eddy momentum flux divergence $\mathcal{T}$ from Equation \ref{eq:T_edd} can is calculated at every grid point. We compare it to the HC strength at the latitude and pressure level of the maximum MSF $\psi_\mathrm{max}$.

\subsection{Applying the energetic framework}
We use Equation \ref{eq:energetic_contributions} to calculate the contributions of changes in forcing, feedback, GMS, eddies, and OHU to changes in energy transport. The contribution is always evaluated at the latitude of the MSF maximum in the PI control run even if it has changed in the forced simulations. This is different than how we usually use it (see Equation \eqref{eq:anomalies}). It guarantees that our decomposition is energetically closed but also leads to slightly different values of HC changes compared to the previous analyses. To calculate the forcing on the atmosphere, we consider the net energy input (NEI) in the fixed SST simulations. The NEI can be determined by evaluating the net TOA fluxes and subtracting the net surface fluxes. It quantifies how much energy has to be transported by the atmosphere due to the direct effect of \CO.
