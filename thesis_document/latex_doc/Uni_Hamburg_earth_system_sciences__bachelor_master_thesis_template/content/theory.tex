In this chapter, we discuss how greenhouse gas forcing is changing the temperature structure and the circulation the atmosphere. Thereafter, we introduce the axisymmetric and eddy-driven HC dynamic framework. Combining the expected temperature structure changes, we are trying to develop a framework to understand the HC response to our non-uniform \CO forcing experiments. Finally, we introduce the energy budget decomposition framework which will help us in the analysis of the relative contributions of changes in the energy balance to changes in the Hadley circulation. 
\section{CO2 forcing in the atmosphere and its temperature response} \label{sec:CO2_T_response}
\CO{} is relatively well mixed in the atmosphere. In our simulations with a general circulation model though, we can prescribe different vertical \CO{} profiles. Since \CO{} is a greenhouse gas, this changes the radiative balance and thus the temperature profile of the atmosphere. This section is describing the temperature response of the atmosphere to a uniform increase in \CO{} concentration and to provide an expectation for the temperature change in the different vertical \CO{} profiles. In particular, we care about the tropopause height, and meridional and vertical temperature gradients, which are important to understand changes in the HC strength and extent as we will show in Section \ref{sec:axisymmetric}.
\subsection{Tropospheric warming and stratospheric cooling} \label{subsec:theo_warming_cooling}
The temperature response to a uniform increase in \CO{} concentration is characterized by a warming in the troposphere and a cooling in the stratosphere. The vertical temperature profile of the atmosphere can be determined as a first approximation from the budget of incoming and outgoing radiation everywhere. This state is called radiative equilibrium (RE).
However, in RE temperature rapidly decreases with height, rendering the atmosphere statically unstable. In reality, convection transports additional energy upward and relaxes the temperature profile to a moist adiabat, with a lapse rate of about \SI{6.5}{\K\per\km}. The resulting atmospheric state is called to be in radiative-convective equilibrium \parencite{manabeThermalEquilibriumAtmosphere1964}. Thus, energy balance in the atmosphere is not just achieved by radiative balances but heavily influenced by convective redistribution of heat. Increasing the \CO concentration in the atmosphere raises the effective emission height in the \CO band from the surface to the stratosphere. Less energy leaves the atmosphere and stays trapped in the troposphere. This increases the temperature in the atmosphere until it is again in radiative balance. On the vertical the temperature change is redistributed via convection \parencite{jeevanjeeClimateSensitivityRadiativeconvective2023}. 
% Solving RE with a numerical transfer code for a non-grey atmosphere, which means including the vertical distribution of radiatively active gases, yields a lapse rate larger than observed in the atmosphere 
% \parencite{manabeThermalEquilibriumAtmosphere1964}. The observed lapse rate on Earth is close to \SI{6.5}{\K\per\km}. It can be shown that the RE temperature profile is highly sensitive to vertical perturbations, so it is convectively unstable. To account for the upward motions and the condensational heating (moist and dry convection), the concept of radiative convective equilibrium (RCE) was introduced. Here, we adjust the lapse rate to the critical lapse rate (stability threshold), whenever it is exceeded in the integrations of the radiative transfer code.
% This model was famously extended by assuming fixed relative humidity (RH) within the troposphere \parencite{manabeThermalEquilibriumAtmosphere1967}. This is a good approximation because the absolute humidity in the atmosphere strongly depends on temperature, so does the saturation water pressure. This model is very successful in explaining the atmosphere's response to increased greenhouse gas emissions. It provided the first realistic estimate of climate sensitivity.
%  The climate sensitivity measures how sensitive the global surface temperature is to changes in \CO{} concentration. The equilibrium climate sensitivity (ECS) for a doubling of \CO{} are estimated to \SIrange{2.5}{5.1}{\K} with a mean value of \SI{3.4}{\K} \parencite{intergovernmentalpanelonclimatechangeipccClimateChange20212023}. An increase in \CO{} concentration in the atmosphere decreases the outgoing longwave radiation (OLR) in the troposphere or moves the emission height as seen from TOA within the \CO{} band from the surface to the stratosphere \parencite{jeevanjeeClimateSensitivityRadiativeconvective2023}. Since the temperatures in the stratosphere are lower than at the surface, less radiation leaves the troposphere. The troposphere is warming. This affects the tropopause height by pushing it upward \parencite{vallisResponseLargescaleStructure2015}, strengthening and widening the HC according to the axisymmetric scalings. 

The temperature in the stratosphere is governed by the relation of heating due to the absorption of shortwave radiation by ozone and the emission of longwave radiation due to \CO{}. One of the most robust responses of increasing the \CO{} concentration in the atmosphere has been the cooling of the stratosphere \parencite{santerSearchHumanInfluences1996}. A qualitative explanation goes like this: Because the heating due to ozone stays constant but the emissivity of \CO{} is increased, the atmosphere has to cool locally to stay in radiative balance. Since the stratosphere adjust faster, the stratospheric cooling also occurs within shorter time scales than the warming in the troposphere. Near the tropopause changes in stratospheric temperature are almost negligible, while they increase with height until they reach their maximum approximately at the stratopause. Here, each doubling of \CO{} decreases the temperature by around \SI{8}{\K}. More insights into the structure of stratospheric cooling can be obtained from a spectroscopic analysis of \CO{} forcing in the atmosphere \parencite{cohenStratosphericCoolingAmplification2026}.

An increase in tropopause height due to a uniform increase of \CO{} is robust at all latitudes in most models. A simple model for a grey atmosphere predicts that the HC changes either due to changes in surface temperature or in lapse rate. Increasing the surface temperature and decreasing the lapse rate pushes the tropopause up \parencite{vallisResponseLargescaleStructure2015}. This is what happens under uniform \CO{} forcing. We would expect the same response for a tropospheric \CO{} increase. For a stratospheric \CO{} increase, we would expect no response in the tropopause height, since surface temperature and the lapse rate are not affected by stratospheric cooling.

Separating the \CO{} concentration change into a tropospheric and a stratospheric component will have a very different response in terms of the temperature anomaly field. For an increase of tropospheric \CO{} concentration, we expect a warming signal from the troposphere due to the increased absorption of \CO{}. For an increase of stratospheric \CO{} concentration, we expect a cooling signal from the stratosphere due to the increased emission. 

% We would expect that tropospheric warming increases the tropopause height because of the thermal expansion of the air within the troposphere
% Since the tropopause is higher at the equator than at the poles with a strong decrease at the location of the subtropical jet, cooling the stratosphere leads to an increased meridional temperature difference here. This increase strengthens the jet increasing the eddies and pushing the zones of baroclinic instability equatorward. If we consider the HC as eddy driven from an angular momentum perspective this would increase the strength of the HC and squeeze it. The interpretation from the energetic perspective is contradictory: The eddies are performing more meridional energy transport, so the HC has to do less given the same amount of energy that has to be transported by the atmosphere.
\subsection{Warming structure and feedbacks}
The warming and cooling pattern in the atmosphere due to a \CO{} increase does not yet reveal how vertical and meridional temperature gradients change. In the tropics the warming aloft is amplified (top heavy). This is due to an increased moisture content, which reduces the lapse rate. This constitutes a negative feedback. Due to the greenhouse gases a substantial fraction of OLR originates in the mid troposphere, where now the warming is amplified, which increases the OLR (Stefan-Boltzmann law). Another important paradigm to explain the horizontal profiles of temperature within the tropics is the weak temperature gradient approximation \parencite{sobelWeakTemperatureGradient2001}. It argues that the Coriolis force is too weak in the tropics to sustain horizontal gradients of pressure or temperature. Diabatic heat fluxes are communicated throughout the tropics by gravity waves maintaining the horizontal homogeneity. These two concepts combined provide a good framework to understand the warming structure with an of \CO{} concentration in the tropical troposphere: The vertical temperature gradient is reduced which means that the atmosphere is more stable. Vertical temperature gradients within the tropics are expected to remain equal due to to the weak temperature gradient approximation.

% Warming the tropopause uniformly in the tropics, increases the meridional temperature gradient at the location of the subtropical jet stream. Similar as with stratospheric cooling, this decreases the HC strength according to the energy budget framework. An increase would be expected for an eddy-driven HC. The HC width is expected to decrease with the equatorward shift of the zones of baroclinic instability. Additionally, the warming is enhanced aloft. This increases the vertical stability of the atmosphere. From the axisymmetric scaling, one would expect a weakening of the circulation. This agrees with the energetic framework, where an increase of GMS enhanced the transport efficiency requiring a less strong circulation given the same amount of energy that has to be transported. 

In the Arctic, we have the somewhat opposite pattern. Warming is amplified at the surface (bottom heavy), and generally larger than at the rest of the globe. This phenomenon is called Arctic amplification. Enhanced warming is neither yet expected nor observed in the Antarctic, where it is delayed by enhanced ocean heat uptake \parencite{armourSouthernOceanWarming2016} and the large elevation of Antarctic continental land mass \parencite{hahnAntarcticElevationDrives2020}. Warming in the Arctic is enhanced during the winter months not during the summer as one would expect from the ice albedo feedback. The enhanced sea ice melt during summer increases the OLR and heat fluxes from the newly opened waters, warming the atmosphere \parencite{daiArcticAmplificationCaused2019}. This local effect is enhanced by remote influence on the local lapse rate. Climatologically, there is a surface-based inversion because advective moist heating keeps the atmosphere warm compared to the surface which strongly cools to space. The warming associated with the sea ice loss weakens the inversion, producing a positive lower lapse rate feedback. Generally, the atmosphere in the Arctic is quite dry and due to inversion convection cannot transport moisture into the free troposphere. This diminishes the upper lapse rate feedback in the Arctic decreasing the OLR relative to lower latitudes, further warming the Arctic \parencite{feldlSeaIceAtmospheric2020}. The enhanced warming at lower level in the Arctic compared to the global mean, increases the meridional near-surface temperate gradient.

\section{Historical and projected Hadley cell trends} \label{sec:HC_proj}
The HC response to a uniform increase in \CO{} concentration and the associated temperature response is very well described in the literature. HC changes have been observed already since the beginning of the satellite era. Observational datasets using outgoing longwave radiation, precipitation, and sea level pressure as proxies for the HC position have shown a significant widening of the HC in the historical period (1979-2000s) \parencite{huObservationalEvidencePoleward2011}. Depending on the observed quantity the widening has a magnitude of \SIrange{1.2}{4.0}{\degree}. Similar trends have also been found in reanalysis data \parencite{huObservedPolewardExpansion2007}. 
In phase 6 of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP6) "All-forcing" experiments also found both Hadley cells widening during the period 1970-2014 by in mean $\SI{0.065}{\degree} \pm \SI{0.02}{\degree}$ per decade. TODO This is much smaller than the trends obtained from observational records. The influence of natural variability is highlighted in this context. The widening in CMIP6 models is found to be larger in the southern hemisphere. The primary driver of the historical widening is greenhouse gases forcing. Changes in anthropogenic aerosol forcing and stratospheric ozone only contribute to southern hemisphere HC changes \parencite{xiaComparisonTrendsHadley2020}.

In terms of the HC strength, studies found opposing trends in climate models and reanalysis data in the historical period for the northern hemisphere. While CMIP5 models show a robust weakening of the NH HC, the reanalysis data even suggests a strengthening. This discrepancy is not due to internal variability but rather stems from a misrepresentation of latent heating in the reanalysis \parencite{chemkeOppositeTropicalCirculation2019}. According to CMIP6 models, greenhouse gas forcing leads to a weakening in both hemispheres, with stratospheric ozone, and aerosol forcing contributing to a net modest strengthening in the southern hemisphere. This strengthening trend had not been observed in the previous CMIP5 models. The annual mean northern hemisphere HC weakened by approximately $5\%$ over 1970-2014 \parencite{xiaComparisonTrendsHadley2020}. 

CMIP6 projections of the HC extent show that the widening trend of the HC increases with radiative forcing in both hemispheres. This increase is stronger in the annual mean southern hemisphere HC while showing no seasonality. The strength of the SH HC is not projected to change with radiative forcing. In the NH, there is a projected net weakening of the annual-mean maximum of about $10\%$ over the period of 2016-2100 \parencite{xiaComparisonTrendsHadley2020}. Consistent with the historical simulation the largest contributor to the change is expected to be the increase in future \CO{} concentrations in the atmosphere. 
\section{Dynamical theories of the Hadley circulation}
% Considering the angular momentum budget of the Earth, one addresses the fundamental question of why there is a meridional circulation in the atmosphere in the first place. An answer is provided by the axisymmetric theory of \textcite{heldNonlinearAxiallySymmetric1980}. It also provides a scaling for the HC strength and extent linking it directly to thermodynamical parameters. Because this theory ignores eddies, we are further investigating their effect on the HC in the following subsection. Since there is no unifying theory incorporating angular momentum conservation and angular momentum transport by baroclinic eddies, we introduce the energetic budget framework of the HC to explain why it changes. This is a more diagnostic framework which does not explain the existence of the HC from first principles but rather takes the necessity transport energy poleward as granted. This necessity can be inferred from observational data of the TOA radiative imbalance showing an excess of incoming radiative fluxes relative to the outgoing radiation at the equator and vice versa at higher latitudes. This transport by the atmosphere can be split up into a mean component, i.e., the HC in the tropics, and eddy component.
Given the temperature responses to a tropospheric, or stratospheric \CO{} forcing, we want to discuss their implications on the Hadley circulation. Thus, we investigate what HC changes are to be expected according to different HC frameworks.  The conservation of angular momentum sets the physical concept to understand the movements of fluids, the circulation of air, in the atmosphere imposed by a thermal structure. In the following subsections, we will introduce two different dynamical theories of the HC and discuss their respective predictions for HC strength and extent changes.

\subsection{Axisymmetric theory}\label{sec:axisymmetric}
Trying to answer the question, why there is a meridional circulation in the first place \textcite{heldNonlinearAxiallySymmetric1980} developed the axisymmetric theory of the HC. This question is raised because one could imagine a world where the Earth is just in radiative equilibrium (see Section \ref{subsec:theo_warming_cooling}) everywhere. This means that every point on Earth emits just as much radiation as it receives from the sun. Thus, there would not be any meridional heat flux necessary. 
The axisymmetric theory considers the zonal angular momentum equation in the $\phi$--$p$ plane with no meridional circulation ($v=\omega=0$). Being zonally symmetric prohibits the existence of large scale barotropic or baroclinic eddies. Choosing a radiative-equilibrium temperature profile, which resembles that of the Earth,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\theta_E}{\theta_0}=1-\frac{1}{3}\Delta_h(3\sin^2\phi-1)+\Delta_v\left(\frac{z}{H}-\frac{1}{2}\right)
\end{equation}
(see Figure \ref{fig:held_hou_T}), one can obtain a corresponding zonal wind $u_E$ via the thermal wind relationship. Here, $\Delta_h$ is the fractional pole to equator temperature difference, $\Delta_v$ the fractional temperature difference between the surface and the top of the fluid, $H$ the depth of the fluid, and $\theta_0$ the global mean value of $\theta_E$. This solution not only produces westerly winds at the equator, which are not observed in the atmosphere, but also violate Hide's theorem \parencite{hideDynamicsAtmospheresMajor1969}. Hide's theorem states that a maximum of angular momentum within the flow domain cannot be sustained due to dissipation within the flow. A maximum of angular momentum can only exist at the boundaries. This tells us that this RE temperature profile cannot persist on Earth and that we require some poleward transport of heat to flatten the meridional temperature gradient.

The conservation of angular momentum
\begin{equation}
    M=(u+\Omega a\cos\phi)a\cos\phi
\end{equation}
provides a second constraint for a HC solution with the zonal wind $u$, $a$ being the radius and $\Omega$ the rotational frequency of the Earth. The angular momentum conserving wind can then be derived as
\begin{equation}
    u_M=\Omega a\frac{\sin^2\phi}{\cos\phi}.
\end{equation}
This allows us to infer, via the thermal wind relationship, a temperature profile $\theta_M$ (see Figure \ref{fig:held_hou_T}), which is now slaved to angular momentum conservation. Assuming that the energy is conserved in the upper branch and that the solution is continuous at the edge of the HC, allows us to determine the undefined parameters of this solution, one of which is the latitude of the HC edge 
\begin{align}\label{eq:HH_ext}
    \phi_H&\sim\left(\frac{gH\Delta_h}{\Omega^2a^2}\right)^{1/2}.
\end{align}
From the temperature equation we also obtain a scaling for its strength
\begin{align}\label{eq:HH_strength}
    \psi_\mathrm{max} &\sim \frac{1}{\tau}\frac{g^{3/2}(H\Delta_h)^{5/2}}{\Delta_v a^2\Omega^3}.
\end{align}
Here, $g$ is the gravitational acceleration of Earth and $\tau$ is the time scale at which $\theta$ relaxes to $\theta_E$.

The key contribution of this theory is to explain why there is a HC with finite width in the first place. It is to reduce the meridional temperature gradients imposed by solar insolation.
%This means that a meridional circulation must exist and it does not extend from the equator to the poles.

For a uniform increase of \CO{} we know that the tropopause height increases, which would lead to a HC widening and strengthening according to Equations \eqref{eq:HH_ext} and \eqref{eq:HH_strength}. The vertical temperature gradient of potential temperature increases (more stable atmosphere), which would lead to a weakening of the HC according to Equation \eqref{eq:HH_strength}. The meridional temperature gradient is expected to decrease at lower levels in the atmosphere due to arctic amplification while it is expected to increase aloft due to stratospheric cooling on the poleward flank of the subtropical jet and the tropospheric warming at the equatorward flank. Thus, we cannot use the axisymmetric theory to qualitatively predict the HC response to a uniform increase of \CO{} in the atmosphere.

For an increase of tropospheric \CO{}, we expect a warming of the troposphere, which enhanced aloft and amplified in the arctic. This will increase the tropopause height, decrease the vertical temperature gradient, and decrease the meridional temperature gradient at lower levels while increasing it aloft. This reflects the same situation as for a uniform increase of \CO{}, which is why we expect a weakening and a widening of the HC (as is evident in the uniform case). Just from the scaling laws of \eqref{eq:HH_ext} and \eqref{eq:HH_strength} we cannot predict the qualitative change of the HC with an increase of tropospheric \CO{}.

For an increase of stratospheric \CO{}, we expect a cooling of the stratosphere. In our axisymmetric framework this only affects the meridional temperature gradient, which is increased at the location of the subtropical jet. From this we would expect a modest widening and strengthening from the axisymmetric scalings if any effect at all. 

The axisymmetric theory predicts that the HC depends directly on thermal driving via the scaling laws. Subsequent studies have advanced this theory also to temperature profiles which have their maximum off the equator, in order to account for seasonal variations \parencite{lindzenHadleyCirculationsZonally1988}. In the axisymmetric model, the HC is weaker in equinox conditions than observed, leading to the suggestion that the HC in the annual mean is not close to equinox conditions but rather that the HC flips between solstitial states. More recent studies have addressed this discrepancy using eddy permitting models \parencite{walkerResponseIdealizedHadley2005}. In these models, the strength of an annually averaged Hadley circulation is comparable to the Hadley circulation driven by an annually averaged heating. This means that the HC in its equinox state resembles very much an annual mean HC. This highlights the importance of eddies in driving the HC strength, which will be covered in the next section.  


\subsection{Eddy driven mean circulation}\label{sec:theo_eddy}
The importance of eddies in shaping the HC was acknowledged even before the axisymmetric theory was developed \parencite{kuoFORCEDFREEMERIDIONAL1956}. More recent studies have compared axisymmetric models to eddy permitting simulations to determine the importance of eddies in altering HC strength \parencite{walkerEddyInfluencesHadley2006,singhEddyInfluencesStrength2017}. They have found that the eddies increase the HC strength by 50--75\%, depending on the model.

From the eddy perspective the HC edge is set by the latitude at which the zonal winds become baroclinically unstable. Baroclinic instability arises due to large vertical shear in a flow. This is the source of eddies. Using a two layer model, one can derive the HC edge as \parencite{Held2000}
\begin{equation}
    \phi_H\sim\left(\frac{NH_e}{\Omega a}\right)^{1/2}
\end{equation}
with $N$ being the Brunt-Väisälä frequency and $H_e$ the depth of the troposphere in the extratropical regions. It is important to note that this is a different scaling than the one derived from the axisymmetric theory. The eddy perspective predicts that the HC edge is set by extratropical parameters, whereas the axisymmetric theory predicts that it is set by tropical parameters.

To quantify HC strength changes from the eddy perspective, we apply a Reynolds decomposition on the zonal and meridional wind. We obtain the eddies as deviations in time from the zonal and temporal mean flow
\begin{equation}
    u=\bar{u}+u',\quad v=\bar{v}+v'.
\end{equation}
These deviations are associated with weather and Rossby waves in the mid-latitudes.
Inserting this into the zonal momentum equation and neglecting viscous forces and vertical advection, we obtain 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:eddy_divergence}
    f\bar{v}(1-Ro)=\frac{1}{a\cos^2\phi}\frac{\partial (\cos^2\phi\ \overline{u'v'})}{\partial\phi} + \frac{\partial\overline{u'\omega'}}{\partial p}. 
\end{equation}
Here, the Rossby number is 
\begin{align}
    Ro=\frac{\bar{\xi}}{f}
\end{align}
where $\xi=\hat{k}\cdot\nabla\times\vec{u}$ is the relative vorticity.
Equation \eqref{eq:eddy_divergence} has two limits. For $Ro\to 1$, the left-hand side becomes zero and the eddy term becomes negligible. This means that the Hadley circulation is close to angular momentum conservation
\begin{align}
    \frac{\partial \overline{M}}{\partial \phi} = 0
\end{align}
as described by the axisymmetric solution in Section \ref{sec:axisymmetric}. For $Ro\to 0$, the meridional wind $\bar{v}$ is instead balanced by the eddy momentum flux convergence. This regime can be understood as eddy-driven (see Figure \ref{fig:eddy_driven_HC}). For the intermediate $Ro$, no complete dynamical theory exists \parencite{schneiderGeneralCirculationAtmosphere2006}.

    \caption{Schematic of the eddy driven HC, from \parencite{vallisAtmosphericOceanicFluid2017}. The eddy momentum fluxes generated in the baroclinic zone transport momentum northward, inducing a mean circulation.  }
    \label{fig:eddy_driven_HC}
\end{wrapfigure}
In the eddy driven limit, the climatological divergence of eddy momentum flux, which is observed in the tropics, requires a positive mean meridional wind. This holds even if the zones of baroclinic instability lie well poleward of the HC edge because Rossby waves can propagate into the HC, producing a poleward momentum flux. At the HC edge there is $\bar{v}=0$ and thus no eddy momentum flux. The eddy momentum flux divergence also explains the equatorward meridional wind in the eddy-driven Ferrel cell in mid-latitudes. Over the seasonal cycle, the Rossby number varies between 0.2 and 0.8, approaching either of the two limits. During the equinoxes the Rossby number is small which indicates a strong eddy influence. During the solstices the Rossby number is closer to $1$ in the winter HC but still small in the summer HC.

Assuming that the HC is entirely eddy driven ($Ro=0$), the HC strength can be predicted from the eddy momentum flux by inserting Equation \eqref{eq:eddy_divergence} into Equation \eqref{eq:msf}. Following \textcite{walkerEddyInfluencesHadley2006}, this gives the integrated eddy momentum flux divergence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:T_edd}
    \psi_\mathrm{max}=\frac{2\pi a\cos\phi}{fg}\int^0_{p_\mathrm{max}}\left(\frac{1}{a\cos^2\phi}\frac{\partial (\cos^2\phi\ \overline{u'v'})}{\partial\phi} + \frac{\partial\overline{u'\omega'}}{\partial p}\right)\mathrm{d}p'\equiv \mathcal{T} 
\end{equation}
with $p_\mathrm{max}$ being the pressure level at which the MSF becomes maximum and the Coriolis parameter $f$. The quantity $\mathcal{T}$ is a predictor for the HC strength given a certain eddy momentum flux divergence. The HC is thus only indirectly coupled to thermodynamic drivers, which set the zones of baroclinic instability. It has been shown to be skillfully predict the HC strength in eddy permitting models for various climates \parencite{walkerEddyInfluencesHadley2006}. 

From the eddy framework, one can predict how the temperature response to \CO{} influences the HC. Therefore, it is crucial to look at how the zones of baroclinic instability change. This is set by the changes in meridional temperature gradient via the thermal wind relationship. The decrease in meridional temperature gradient at the surface opposes the effect of an increase in meridional temperature gradient aloft in both the uniform and the tropospheric \CO{} increase case. Which effect dominates the jet change, is still discussed in the literature \parencite{ossoAdvancingOurUnderstanding2024}.

\section{The energy budget framework of the Hadley circulation}\label{sec:theo_energy}
The energy budget analysis provides a diagnostic perspective on the HC. It cannot explain why the Earth is not in radiative (convective) equilibrium, but provides a framework for understanding its energy transport mechanisms. Thus, it circumvents the Rossby number regime dependency, providing a more universal approach. Important to notice is that this framework can only deliver a prognostic relationship between energy transport and HC strength. Changes in HC extent cannot be investigated.

Using TOA radiative fluxes (see Figure \ref{fig:irradiance}), one can infer that the short-wave radiative flux at the equator is much larger than the emitted outgoing long wave radiation. The opposite is true for the poles. This implies a poleward energy transport by the atmosphere and the ocean. 
\begin{figure}[h]
    %% trim=left bottom right top,
    \centering
   \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth,keepaspectratio,trim=0 0 0 0, clip]{initial_plots/irradiance.png}
    \caption{Zonal and annual mean average of net shortwave and net outgoing longwave radiation at the TOA from PI control (see Section \ref{sec:setup}) simulations. The sum shows a net surplus of energy in the tropics and an energy deficiency in the extra-tropics, implying a poleward energy transport.}
    \label{fig:irradiance}
\end{figure}
For every column of the earth system, the energy tendency can be written as 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial E_\mathrm{ao}}{\partial t}=R_\mathrm{TOA}-\Delta F_\mathrm{ao}
\end{equation}
where $R_\mathrm{TOA}$ is the net radiative flux at the top of the atmosphere, which consists of the net shortwave radiation plus the net longwave radiation (positive values indicate a downward flux), and $\Delta F_\mathrm{ao}$ is the divergence of energy transport by the atmosphere and ocean \parencite{GlobalPhysicalClimatology2016,oortObservedAnnualCycle1976}.

Integrating this equation in the steady state until latitude $\phi$ gives
\begin{equation}
        \label{eq:Fao}
        F_{ao}(\phi)=\int^\phi_{-\pi/2}\int^{2\pi}_0R'_\mathrm{TOA}a^2\cos\phi\mathrm{d}\lambda\mathrm{d}\phi',
\end{equation}
which provides a measure for the energy that has to pass through this latitude. We use the anomalous net radiative TOA flux $R'_\mathrm{TOA}$ here, subtracting the global mean value, to make sure that the energy transport is closed (zero transport at both poles) \parencite{zelinkaClimateFeedbacksTheir2012}. To separate between the energy transport by the atmosphere $F_a$ and the ocean, one can subtract the surface energy fluxes, which can be obtained from the sum of surface latent, sensible heat, and net shortwave and longwave fluxes, from the TOA radiative fluxes.

To understand the changes in the energy transport are related to changes in the HC, we need to quantify the energy transport by the HC.
For this, we need to determine the energy within an air parcel in the atmosphere, which is given by the moist static energy (MSE). It is defined as
\begin{equation}
    h=c_pT+gz+L_vq
\end{equation}
with $c_p$ being the specific heat capacity at constant pressure, $g$ the gravitational acceleration, $z$ the height, $L_v$ the latent heat of vaporization and $q$ the specific humidity.
The transport of MSE by the mean meridional circulation at a certain latitude $\phi$ is given by vertical integration of the adjusted meridional wind $\bar{v}_\mathrm{adj}$ multiplied by the MSE $h$, which is the meridional energy flux, over the entire atmospheric column
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F_HC}
    F_\mathrm{HC}(\phi)=\frac{2\pi a\cos\phi}{g}\int^{p_s}_0[\Bar{v}_\mathrm{adj}][\Bar{h}]\mathrm{d}p.
\end{equation}
This expression compares the poleward energy transport by the upper branch of the HC to the equatorward energy transport by the lower branch. The adjusted meridional wind is defined as 
\begin{equation}
    [\bar{v}_\mathrm{adj}] = [\bar{v}_{+}]-[\bar{v}_{-}]\frac{\{[\bar{v}_{+}]\}}{\{[\bar{v}_{-}]\}},
\end{equation}
where $[\bar{v}_{+}]$ denotes northward zonal mean wind (i.e., equal to $[\bar{v}]$ if $[\bar{v}]>0$ and zero elsewhere), and $[\bar{v}_{-}]$ defined conversely with $\{\cdot\}=\int\frac{\cdot}{g}\mathrm{d}p$ indicating a vertical mass weighted integral over the depth of the column. This transformation guarantees that the meridional mass flow is strictly in mass balance with $\{[\bar{v}_\mathrm{adj}]\}=0$. It provides a correction for imbalances produced by the interpolation from sigma to pressure vertical levels. Figure \ref{fig:transport} shows the energy transport by the mean circulation, which is largest in the tropics where it is called the Hadley cell.

Expressing the HC strength $\psi_\mathrm{max}$ in terms of the energy transport $F_\mathrm{HC}$ and the gross moist stability $H$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:F_psi_H}
    F_\mathrm{HC}=\psi_\mathrm{max}H
\end{equation}



Gross moist stability (GMS) is defined as the ratio of energy transport to mass transport by the HC which can be understood as the energy transport efficiency. It is inferred to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:GMS}
    \mathcal{H} (\phi)=\frac{2\pi a\cos\phi}{g}\frac{1}{\psi_\mathrm{max}}\int^{p_s}_0[\Bar{v}_\mathrm{adj}][\Bar{h}]\mathrm{d}p.
\end{equation}
It is a measure for the stratification of the atmosphere. A large GMS indicates a strongly stratified atmosphere.
To understand the changes in HC strength, we linearize Equation \eqref{eq:F_psi_H} and obtain 
\begin{equation}
        \frac{\delta\psi_\mathrm{max}}{\psi_\mathrm{max}}=\frac{\delta F_\mathrm{HC}}{F_\mathrm{HC}}-\frac{\delta \mathcal{H} }{\mathcal{H} }.
\end{equation}
This yields the contribution of GMS and HC transport changes.
Knowing the total energy transport in the atmosphere $F_\mathrm{a}$ from Equation \eqref{eq:Fao}, it can be further split up into a mean $F_\mathrm{HC}$ and an eddy energy flux component $F_\mathrm{e}$ (see Figure \ref{fig:transport}), which gives
\begin{equation}
    F_\mathrm{a}=F_\mathrm{HC}+F_\mathrm{e}.
\end{equation}
The eddy component can be calculated as the difference between $F_\mathrm{a}$ and $F_\mathrm{HC}$ or by using the transient eddy fluxes of MSE
\begin{equation}
    F_\mathrm{e}(\phi)=\frac{2\pi a\cos\phi}{g}\int^{p_s}_0[\overline{v'h'}]\mathrm{d}p'.
\end{equation}
Inserting this into the linearized Equation \eqref{eq:F_HC} gives
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\delta\psi_\mathrm{max}}{\psi_\mathrm{max}}&=\frac{\delta F_\mathrm{a}}{F_\mathrm{HC}}-\frac{\delta F_\mathrm{e}}{F_\mathrm{HC}}-\frac{\delta \mathcal{H} }{\mathcal{H} }.
\end{align*}
The transport by the atmosphere can be expressed as 
\begin{equation}
    F_\mathrm{a}=F_\mathrm{ao}-F_\mathrm{o}    
\end{equation}
with $F_\mathrm{o}$ being the transport due to ocean heat uptake (see Figure \ref{fig:transport}).
The HC changes are now a function of the total change in energy transport. The changes in $R_\mathrm{TOA}$ can be further split up into a change in radiative forcing $R_f$ and a change due to feedbacks $\sum_i\lambda_i\Delta T_s$, which gives 
\begin{align}\label{eq:forc_fdb}
    \Delta R_\mathrm{TOA}&=\sum_i\lambda_i\Delta T_s+R_f.
\end{align}
The forcing is the temperature-independent effect of CO2 on the TOA radiative fluxes and the feedbacks are the changes in TOA radiative fluxes due to changes in surface temperature. This change in surface temperature has an effect on the surface albedo, water vapor, clouds, and lapse rate, and OLR. An effect that with increasing surface temperature increases or decreases the downward radiation is called a positive or negative feedback, respectively.

Inserting Equation \eqref{eq:forc_fdb} into Equation \eqref{eq:Fao} gives us the final expression for the HC strength changes decomposed into the contributions of forcing $\delta F_\mathrm{forc}$, feedbacks $\delta F_\mathrm{fdb}$, ocean heat uptake $F_\mathrm{o}$, eddies $\delta F_\mathrm{e}$, and GMS $\delta H$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energetic_contributions}
        \frac{\delta\psi_\mathrm{max}}{\psi_\mathrm{max}}=\frac{\delta F_\mathrm{forc}}{F_\mathrm{HC}}+\frac{\delta F_\mathrm{fdb}}{F_\mathrm{HC}}-\frac{\delta F_\mathrm{o}}{F_\mathrm{HC}}-\frac{\delta F_\mathrm{e}}{F_\mathrm{HC}}-\frac{\delta H}{H}.
\end{equation}

GMS is expected to increase with global warming, which is according to this framework contributing to a weakening of the HC.

An increase in eddy heat transport also contributes to a decreases in HC strength, because more of the necessary transport carried out by the eddies. In CMIP models, studies find a decrease in OHU at the equator relative to the extra-tropics, which is responsible for a strengthening of the HC \parencite{feldlCharacterizingHadleyCirculation2016}. Due to the meridional forcing structure, which is set by the surface temperature \parencite{jeevanjeeAnalyticalModelSpatially2021} and therefore enhanced in the tropics relative to the extra-tropics, one finds a positive forcing contribution. Using the radiative kernel technique allows us to estimate the influence of the individual climate feedbacks on the HC changes. The contribution of a feedback is determined by its meridional structure not by it being positive or negative, because it is always evaluated against its meridional mean. A feedback that is larger or smaller than its mean value in the tropics compared to the extra-topics or the poles strengthens or weakens the HC, respectively. For a uniform increase in \CO{} concentration this is the case for the water vapor feedback. The lapse rate, Planck and surface albedo feedback contribute to a weakening of the HC. The cloud feedback shows a large model dependence and can either contribute positively or negatively \parencite{feldlCharacterizingHadleyCirculation2016}.

Transferring this to the tropospheric \CO{} case almost all aspects seem identical here. There only might be some uncertainty again regarding the eddy component. This means the HC response should be similar as for a uniform increase of \CO{},i.e., a weakening of the HC. For an increase in stratospheric \CO{}, one would expect no parameter to change significantly, and thus also the HC to stay rather constant. The biggest uncertainty is in the eddy and the forcing component. GMS and the feedback component should be zero because they both only relate to temperature changes in the troposphere, which we do not expect.



\subsection{Other drivers of HC strength changes}
In the previous section we saw three frameworks that could provide physical explanations for HC changes. In the literature, several mechanisms more have been proposed to explain HC weakening with greenhouse gas forcing. There seems to be a 'tug of war' between different mechanisms.

There are two mechanisms relating the weakening of the HC to the direct effect of \CO{} increase in the atmosphere. Direct means only considering \CO{} effect on radiative transfer without its influence surface temperature changes with their corresponding feedbacks.

Around 81\% of the net radiative cooling of the atmosphere is balanced by latent heating which poses a constraint on the precipitation rate \parencite[p. 35]{GlobalPhysicalClimatology2016}. \textcite{bonyRobustDirectEffect2013} argue that by increasing the \CO{} in the atmosphere, the loss of infrared radiation to space is reduced. This warms and stabilizes the atmosphere weakening upward motions, i.e., the circulation. 

The second mechanism employs the spatial structure of \CO{} forcing. In regions of ascending motion, such as the equator, the \CO{}  radiative forcing is reduced. This is due to the presence of deep-convective clouds and the high humidity, which both are effective longwave absorbers. Thus, \CO{} below cloud top or the moist atmosphere has a limited effect on OLR (I think this argument is wrong because \CO{} band vs. water vapor absorption region). In subsiding regions, such as the subtropics, the \CO{} radiative forcing is larger because this effect is missing. Given a larger forcing in the extra-tropics than at the equator, less energy transport and also a weaker circulation is required. About 15\% of the century scale weakening is attributed to the direct effect of \CO{} using fixed-SST simulations \parencite{merlisDirectWeakeningTropical2015}.

Another mechanism that explains the HC weakening compares the change in saturation vapor pressure according to the Clausius-Clapeyron (CC) equation to the changes in precipitation. Assuming that the global mean precipitation is given as 
\begin{equation}
    P = Mq
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the convective mass flux and $q$ is a typical boundary layer mixing ratio, which scales with the CC equation. In its linearized form, one obtains the following expression for the change in mass flux
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\delta M}{M} = \frac{\delta P}{P} -\frac{\delta q}{q}.
\end{equation}
Inserting typical global mean values for ${\delta P}/{P}=0.03\delta T$ (where does this value come from? simulations?) and with CC ${\delta q}/{q}=0.07\delta T$ gives a mass flux change of ${\delta M}/{M}=-0.04\delta T$, a weakening of global circulations
\parencite{heldRobustResponsesHydrological2006}. This relationship holds better for global zonal circulations, e.g., the Walker circulation, than the HC \parencite{vecchiGlobalWarmingWeakening2007}.


% \textcite{seoMechanismFutureChanges2014} used the axisymmetric scaling (see next section) to predict the HC strength changes via changes in the meridional temperature gradient, static stability, and tropopause height. They found the meridional temperature gradient to have the largest correlation. Another correlation study finds a strong link between HC strength and these scaling parameters in the northern hemisphere while emphasizing that all of them are not independent themselves but rather highly correlated \parencite{dagostinoFactorsControllingHadley2017}. 

Similiar to the decomposition of HC changes in the energetic framework, diabatic heating, eddy heat flux, eddy momentum flux, zonal friction and static stability changes can also be seen as the drivers of change in maximum MSF \parencite{vallisResponseLargescaleStructure2015}. None of these factors alone can fully explain the changes but the most important contribution according to this decomposition comes from an increase in static stability with diabatic heating changes even contributing to a strengthening of the HC on longer time scales \parencite{chemkeElucidatingMechanismsResponsible2021}.
For a comprehensive evaluation and comparison of the different proposed HC weakening mechanisms, one should consult
\textcite{chemkeElucidatingMechanismsResponsible2021}.


Correlating the HC edge with tropical tropopause height and extratropical tropopause height in CMIP models has shown that the HC edge is set by the extratropical tropopause height \parencite{luExpansionHadleyCell2007} instead of the tropical tropopause height. This favors an eddy driven perspective of the HC edge.
